  Q: What is overfitting in machine learning?
  A: Overfitting in machine learning occurs when a model learns the training data too well, including its noise, which results in poor generalization to new, unseen data. An overfitted model demonstrates high performance on training samples but fails to accurately predict outcomes for new examples, as it has memorized specific details rather than identifying general patterns. Indicators of overfitting include a significant disparity between training accuracy and validation accuracy. To mitigate overfitting, techniques such as regularization, dropout, early stopping, and increasing the amount of training data can be employed.

  Q: Explain the bias-variance tradeoff.
  A: The bias-variance tradeoff is a key concept in machine learning that describes the balance between two types of errors in a model: bias and variance. High bias leads to underfitting, where the model makes strong assumptions and fails to capture the underlying data patterns, while high variance results in overfitting, where the model is too sensitive to fluctuations in the training data. The goal is to find an optimal model that minimizes total prediction error by balancing both bias and variance.

[04/50] [req-004] [v2]
  Q: How does regularization prevent overfitting?
  A: Regularization prevents overfitting by adding penalty terms to the loss function, which discourages overly complex models. Specifically, L1 regularization (Lasso) promotes sparsity by adding the absolute value of weights, while L2 regularization (Ridge) keeps weights small but non-zero by adding the squared magnitude of weights. This discouragement of complexity helps the model to generalize better to unseen data, rather than memorizing the training data, which is a common issue in overfitting. The strength of regularization is controlled by a hyperparameter, lambda, allowing for flexibility in balancing model complexity and performance.

[06/50] [req-006] [v2]
  Q: What is backpropagation?
  A: Backpropagation is the core algorithm used for training neural networks. It computes the gradients of the loss function with respect to each weight by applying the chain rule of calculus, starting from the output layer and propagating error signals backward through the network. This process involves calculating partial derivatives layer by layer, which indicates how much each weight contributed to the total error. The computed gradients are then used to update the weights through optimization techniques such as gradient descent and its variants, including Adam, RMSprop, and SGD with momentum, to minimize the loss function.

This design enables significant parallelism during training, enhancing efficiency and performance in various NLP tasks.

[29/50] [req-029] [v2]
  Q: What is HNSW?
  A: HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World) is a graph-based algorithm designed for approximate nearest-neighbor search. It constructs a hierarchical graph structure with multiple layers, where higher layers have fewer nodes and longer-range connections, while lower layers contain all nodes with shorter-range connections. This design allows HNSW to perform logarithmic-complexity searches, starting from the top layer and navigating down to finer layers for increased precision. The algorithm is known for providing an excellent balance between search accuracy and speed, making it the default index type in many vector databases.

  Q: What is hybrid search in vector databases?
  A: Hybrid search in vector databases is a method that integrates the advantages of both neural and keyword-based retrieval systems. It combines dense vector search, which excels at semantic matching, with sparse keyword search (such as BM25), which is effective for precise keyword matching. The process utilizes Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) to merge the ranked results from both approaches, enhancing the robustness of the combined ranking by summing the reciprocal ranks. This approach ensures that users benefit from both semantic understanding and exact keyword retrieval in their search results.

[42/50] [req-042] [v2]
  Q: How does RAGAS compute faithfulness?
  A: RAGAS computes faithfulness by extracting claims from the generated answer and verifying whether each claim can be inferred from the retrieved context. It utilizes a language model (LLM) to break down the answer into atomic statements and then checks each statement against the retrieved context. The faithfulness score is calculated as the fraction of statements that are supported by the context, with a target score of 0.8 or higher commonly used as a quality threshold. This approach ensures that the answers are factually grounded and do not include hallucinated facts.

RAGAS employs LLMs to assess the quality of answers, emphasizing their factual grounding and relevance to the retrieved context.
